About

I study the safety of agentic AI systems, focusing on multi-agent evaluation, organizational safety, and interaction-level alignment. My research combines empirical LLM evaluation (Inspect AI, RLHF/RLAIF, and constitutional AI) with tools from economics, game theory, and organization theory to understand how organizational structure, incentives, coordination, and information flow shape agent behavior and amplify risks such as cascading failures, hidden-objective propagation, and emergent coordination.

My long-term research goal is to develop rigorous evaluation frameworks and alignment methods to improve the safety, reliability, and governance of increasingly autonomous AI systems. I am currently an Assistant Professor at the David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, and hold a Ph.D. in Management Science from the University of Pittsburgh.

AI Safety Research

  1. Secret Loyalties in Agentic Organizations Multi-agent evaluation and organizational safety · Lead researcher · Inspect AI · Writeup available on request

    Setup. A controlled evaluation of how a hidden objective, seeded in one compromised agent, propagates through multi-agent LLM organizations under varying delegation rules and information-sharing regimes.

    Implementation. Built centralized and decentralized organizational scaffolds in Inspect AI, with structured transcript logging and custom safety metrics measuring blast radius, task-goal degradation, and hidden-goal completion.

    Findings. Decentralization substantially reduces effective blast radius, while broad information-sharing amplifies hidden-objective spread and emergent coordination even when only one agent is compromised.

    Contribution. Connects scalable-oversight and multi-agent safety research to a design-space perspective on how organizational structure and information flow shape cascading risk in agentic systems.

  2. Interaction-Level Governance for Healthcare LLMs Interaction-level alignment · Lead author · Under review at npj Digital Medicine (Nature)

    Framework. A three-layer governance framework that stratifies each interaction by clinical risk, user state, and decision proximity rather than treating all queries symmetrically. Risk tiers map to response strategies (clarification, escalation, bounded non-directive guidance), addressing the “gray-area” failure mode where models oscillate between sycophancy and blanket refusal.

    Uncertainty mechanism. Proposed inter-model agreement on clinical risk assessment across multiple LLMs as an uncertainty signal for conservative arbitration.

    Ongoing. Investigating RLHF and preference-optimization approaches to operationalize interaction-level safety policies as post-training targets.

  3. Constitutional Training for Role-Based Alignment Interaction-level alignment · Ongoing research

    Question. Can constitutional training mitigate harmful behavior arising from strong persona identification and persistent role context—role-play jailbreaks, sycophancy under persona pressure, goal drift within long conversations?

    Approach. Designing an experimental framework based on the attached-vs-detached role-identification distinction, evaluating whether separating role-playing from goal pursuit improves alignment while preserving task performance. Comparing constitutional and preference-based training regimes across diverse personas.

  4. Honesty–Sycophancy Tradeoff in LLMs with Evolving User Preferences Incentives and trust in agentic systems · Working paper

    A formal model of the strategic tension providers face between honesty (long-run trust) and sycophancy (short-run engagement) when users have limited ability to detect sycophancy and preferences drift with model output. Proposes trust as a benchmark metric for alignment and governance evaluation, and identifies conditions under which honesty is the equilibrium strategy.
